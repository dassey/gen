# Mission analysis

Mission analysis is where planning succeeds or fails. Its output is
understanding: of the higher commander's intent, of the problem, of the
operational environment, and of what the unit must actually do.

## The activities

1. Analyze the higher headquarters' plan or order — two levels up.
2. Perform initial intelligence preparation of the battlefield.
3. Determine specified, implied, and essential tasks.
4. Review available assets and identify resource shortfalls.
5. Determine constraints and restraints.
6. Identify critical facts and develop assumptions.
7. Begin risk management.
8. Develop initial CCIRs and EEFIs.
9. Develop the initial information collection plan.
10. Update the plan for the use of available time.
11. Develop initial themes and messages.
12. Develop a proposed problem statement.
13. Develop a proposed mission statement.
14. Present the mission analysis briefing.
15. Develop and issue the initial commander's intent.
16. Develop and issue initial planning guidance.
17. Develop COA evaluation criteria.
18. Issue a warning order.

## Tasks

A **specified task** is stated in the higher order — in paragraph 2, paragraph 3,
the coordinating instructions, or an annex. Quote it; do not paraphrase it.

An **implied task** is not stated but must be performed to accomplish the
mission. Routine tasks covered by unit SOP are not implied tasks; if the unit
does it every time without being told, it does not belong on the list.

An **essential task** is a specified or implied task that must be executed to
accomplish the mission. Essential tasks appear in the mission statement.

## Constraints and restraints

A **constraint** requires action — "retain one company as a reserve", "attack no
earlier than H-Hour". A **restraint** prohibits action — "do not damage the
bridge", "no fires into the built-up area without approval". Both come from the
higher headquarters and both limit the freedom of the planner.

## Facts and assumptions

A **fact** is a statement of known data. An **assumption** replaces a necessary
fact that is not available. A valid assumption is:

- **Necessary** — planning cannot continue without it.
- **Valid** — it is likely to be true, not merely convenient.
- **Confirmable** — the staff can and will confirm or deny it, usually through
  an information requirement.

A large number of assumptions is a symptom that the plan is not yet grounded.
Each assumption that fails becomes a branch the staff has to plan.

## The mission statement

One sentence containing WHO, WHAT (the task), WHEN, WHERE, and WHY (the
purpose). The task comes from the essential tasks. The purpose comes from the
higher commander's intent. Avoid chains of "in order to" — one purpose.

Example: *2d Brigade Combat Team attacks along AXIS EAGLE at 010500Z to seize
OBJECTIVE FALCON and secure the crossing site in order to enable the division's
exploitation east of the river.*

## The problem statement

One or two sentences naming the gap between the current conditions and the
desired conditions, and what stands in the way. If the problem statement reads
like a restatement of the mission, it is not doing any work.
