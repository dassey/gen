# COA analysis — war gaming

War gaming is a disciplined attempt to break your own plan before the enemy
does. It is not a briefing rehearsal and it is not a confirmation exercise.

## The eight steps

1. Gather the tools — maps, overlays, running estimates, threat templates.
2. List all friendly forces available.
3. List the assumptions in effect.
4. List known critical events and decision points.
5. Select the war-game method.
6. Select a technique to record and display results.
7. War game the operation and assess the results.
8. Conduct the war-game briefing.

## Methods

- **Belt.** Divide the area of operations into belts — usually phases or lateral
  strips across the sector — and war game each in turn. Best when the operation
  is phased or the terrain divides cleanly. Gives the most complete picture of
  synchronization across the force.
- **Avenue-in-depth.** War game one avenue of approach from start to finish,
  then the next. Best for offensive operations, infiltration, and when avenues
  are separated by terrain.
- **Box.** Isolate one critical area — an objective, an engagement area, a river
  crossing — and war game it in detail. Best when time is short or when a single
  event dominates the operation.

## Recording

The **synchronization matrix** is the standard product: time or phase across the
top, warfighting functions and subordinate units down the side. It becomes the
execution matrix. The **sketch-note** method is faster and lower resolution.

## The method itself

Action, reaction, counteraction. The friendly force acts; the threat reacts as a
thinking opponent would; the friendly force counteracts. Record what you learn,
not what you hoped. A war game that produces no findings was not a war game.

Assign someone to fight the threat honestly, and give them the threat template
and the threat's doctrine rather than a script. If the same person plans the
friendly scheme and fights the threat, the plan will win every time and teach
nothing.

## What comes out

- Refined COAs, including changes forced by what the war game revealed.
- Decision points with triggers and latest times.
- The synchronization matrix.
- Refined CCIRs — the war game usually shows which information actually drives
  decisions.
- Updated risk assessment.
- Task organization adjustments.
